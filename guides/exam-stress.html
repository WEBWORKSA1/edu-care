Exam Stress: Practical Techniques That Help Students

Some nerves before an exam are normal and can even sharpen focus. When stress starts to block recall or sleep, these techniques help bring it back to a useful level.

By the Edu.Care Editorial Team · Reviewed November 2026

Why stress hurts recall

Under strong stress, attention narrows and working memory, the mental space you use to hold and manipulate information, has less capacity. That is why students sometimes “go blank” on material they knew the night before. The goal is not zero stress; it is keeping stress in a range where you can still think clearly.

In the weeks before

  • Practice under test conditions. Timed practice papers make the real exam feel familiar, which reduces threat.
  • Use retrieval practice. Knowledge that has been recalled many times is more resistant to stress. See our study schedule guide.
  • Protect sleep. Consistent sleep supports memory consolidation and mood.
  • Move every day. Even a brisk 20-minute walk can lift mood and ease tension.
  • Plan the logistics (location, materials, transport) so nothing is left to chance.

The night before and the morning of

  • Do a light review of key points, not new material.
  • Pack everything you need the night before.
  • Eat a normal breakfast and arrive early enough to settle, but avoid crowds of anxious classmates comparing notes.

During the exam

  1. Slow breathing. Breathe in for about four counts and out for about six. Longer exhales help your body shift toward a calmer state.
  2. Brain dump. Spend the first minute writing down formulas, dates or frameworks you are worried about forgetting.
  3. Skim and start with a confident question to build momentum.
  4. If you go blank, move on, mark the question and return later. Answers often surface once the pressure drops.
  5. Reframe the feeling. Telling yourself “this is energy to help me focus” can work better than trying to force calm.
Try expressive writing: some studies found that students who wrote about their worries for about ten minutes before an exam performed better than those who did not. It can help offload anxious thoughts.

How parents can help

  • Ask “what would help you right now?” instead of “have you studied enough?”
  • Keep routines, meals and sleep steady during exam periods.
  • Praise effort and strategy, not just results.
  • Keep perspective: one exam rarely decides a future, and there are almost always second chances.

When to seek support

If anxiety is causing panic attacks, persistent sleep problems, avoiding school, or feelings of hopelessness, talk to a school counselor, doctor or mental health professional. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact local emergency services.
