Online Degrees and Certificates: Checking Accreditation and ROI

Online learning has made degrees and career credentials far more accessible. It has also made it easier to overpay for qualifications employers do not value. Check these five things before you enroll.

By the Edu.Care Editorial Team · Reviewed November 2026

1. Accreditation comes first

Accreditation means an independent body recognized by government education authorities has reviewed the institution. It affects whether employers recognize the qualification, whether credits transfer, and whether you can use government financial aid. In the United States, check the U.S. Department of Education’s database of accredited institutions or the Council for Higher Education Accreditation. Other countries maintain official registers through their education ministries or quality agencies. Verify on the official register itself, not just on the school’s website.

Watch for “accreditation mills”: organizations with official-sounding names that accredit schools for a fee. If you cannot find the accreditor on a government-recognized list, treat the program as unaccredited.

3. Calculate the full cost

Add tuition, fees, technology costs, books, exam fees and any in-person residency travel. Ask whether the price is per credit or per term and whether it rises each year. Compare with the same program’s on-campus price; online is not always cheaper.

4. Look for real outcomes

  • Graduation or completion rates for online students specifically.
  • Job placement rates, with how they are measured.
  • Typical salaries for graduates in your region and field.
  • Talk to two or three alumni; LinkedIn makes this easy.

5. A simple ROI check

Estimate the extra yearly income the credential could realistically bring. Divide the total cost by that amount: the result is your payback period in years. A payback under three to five years is generally strong for career-focused programs. If you will borrow, use our student loan calculator to see how interest changes the picture.

Red flags

  • High-pressure enrollment calls and “discounts that expire today.”
  • Credit for “life experience” alone, with little coursework.
  • Degrees awarded in weeks.
  • Vague or missing information about faculty, accreditation or refund policies.

Make online learning work

Online students who succeed typically schedule fixed study blocks, join study groups or forums, and talk to instructors early. Our guide to building a study schedule applies at any age.
