How to Calculate Your GPA: Weighted vs. Unweighted, Explained

Your grade point average turns a year of report cards into one number that colleges, scholarship committees and employers read in seconds. Here is exactly how it is calculated, and how to move it.

By the Edu.Care Editorial Team · Reviewed November 2026

What a GPA actually measures

A GPA is a credit-weighted average. Each letter grade is converted to grade points, multiplied by the credits (or hours) the course is worth, added up, and divided by the total credits. A two-credit class therefore counts twice as much as a one-credit class. Most US high schools and colleges use a 4.0 scale; some schools use 5.0, 10-point or percentage systems, so always check your own school’s conversion table.

Some schools award 4.3 for an A+. Pass/fail, withdrawn and incomplete courses are usually left out of the calculation.

Step-by-step: a worked example

  1. List each course with its credits and grade. English (1 credit, A-), Algebra II (1, B+), Chemistry (1, B), History (1, A), Art (0.5, A).
  2. Convert grades to points and multiply by credits. 3.7 + 3.3 + 3.0 + 4.0 + (4.0 × 0.5 = 2.0) = 16.0 quality points.
  3. Add the credits. 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0.5 = 4.5.
  4. Divide. 16.0 ÷ 4.5 = 3.56 unweighted GPA.

Weighted GPA

Weighted GPAs reward harder courses. A common convention adds 0.5 points for honors classes and 1.0 point for AP, IB or dual-enrollment classes, so an A in AP Chemistry becomes 5.0 instead of 4.0. Weighting conventions differ widely: some schools cap the bonus, some weight only junior and senior years, and many colleges recalculate every applicant’s GPA with their own formula. That is why admissions officers look at the rigor of your transcript, not just the headline number.

Cumulative vs. term GPA

Your term GPA covers one semester. Your cumulative GPA combines every term, weighted by credits. Because the denominator keeps growing, each new semester moves your cumulative GPA less than the last one. Raising a 2.8 to a 3.2 in your final year takes far more effort than doing it in your first year, which is the strongest argument for taking ninth grade and first-year college courses seriously.

Seven practical ways to raise your GPA

  • Fix the biggest-credit course first. One grade step in a four-credit lab course moves the average more than in a one-credit elective.
  • Know the grading weights. If homework is 20% and you are missing assignments, that is the fastest recovery point.
  • Use retrieval practice rather than re-reading. See our guide to building a study schedule that works.
  • Ask for feedback early, not after the test. Office hours are underused and highly effective.
  • Calculate what you need on the final with the final grade calculator so you study with a target.
  • Check retake and grade-replacement policies; some schools replace an earlier grade when you repeat a course.

Does GPA matter as much as people think?

GPA is one of the strongest single predictors that colleges use, because it reflects years of consistent work rather than a single test day. But context matters: an upward trend, a demanding course load and strong teacher recommendations can offset a weaker start. For many scholarships, GPA is a minimum gate (for example, “3.0 or higher”) rather than the deciding factor, so clearing the bar opens more doors than chasing the last tenth of a point.
