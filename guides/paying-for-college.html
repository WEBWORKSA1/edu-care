Paying for College: Grants, Scholarships, Work-Study and Loans Explained

The sticker price is rarely what families pay. Use funding sources in the right order and compare offers by net cost, and college gets far more affordable.

By the Edu.Care Editorial Team · Reviewed November 2026

The funding order that saves the most

  1. Free money first: grants and scholarships do not need to be repaid.
  2. Earned money: work-study, part-time jobs, savings and family contributions.
  3. Government student loans: usually lower and fixed rates, with more flexible repayment protections than private loans.
  4. Private loans last: compare carefully, and only for the remaining gap.

Grants

Grants are need-based awards from governments, states or provinces, and colleges themselves. In the United States, the gateway is the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid), which determines eligibility for federal grants and is used by many states and colleges for their own aid. Other countries have equivalents, such as provincial aid in Canada and the National Scholarship Portal in India. File as early as possible: some state and institutional funds are first-come, first-served.

Scholarships

Scholarships can be merit-based, need-based or tied to a field, background or community. Colleges are often the biggest source, so check each school’s scholarship page and deadlines. Outside scholarships fill the remaining gap; our scholarship playbook shows how to find and win them.

Work-study and part-time work

Work-study programs offer part-time jobs, often on campus, with schedules that fit classes. Research suggests moderate part-time work (roughly 10 to 15 hours a week) is manageable for most students, while heavy work hours can hurt grades and graduation timelines.

Loans: borrow with a number in mind

A widely used rule of thumb: keep total student debt at or below your expected first-year salary in your intended field. Before borrowing, estimate your monthly payment with our student loan calculator.

How to compare financial aid offers

Offers are not standardized, and some list loans next to grants as if they were the same thing. For each school, calculate:

Net price = total cost of attendance − grants and scholarships.

Cost of attendance includes tuition, fees, housing, food, books, transport and personal costs. Then look separately at how much of the net price would be covered by loans versus savings and earnings. Ask whether scholarships renew each year and what GPA is required.

Appeal if your situation changed

If your family’s finances changed because of job loss, medical costs or other circumstances, contact the financial aid office and ask about a professional judgment review. Be polite, specific and provide documentation. Competing offers from similar schools can sometimes help too.

Start saving early

Even small monthly contributions grow over time. Use the college savings calculator to see what a goal would take per month.
