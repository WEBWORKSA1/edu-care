Supporting a Child With Learning Differences at School

Learning differences such as dyslexia, dyscalculia and ADHD are common, and children who get the right support early can thrive. Here is how to move from worry to a plan.

By the Edu.Care Editorial Team · Reviewed November 2026

Important: this guide is general information for families. Only qualified professionals can evaluate or diagnose a child. If you have concerns, speak with your child’s school and health care provider.

Signs worth paying attention to

Every child develops at their own pace, so one sign on its own rarely means much. Patterns that persist over months, across settings, are more meaningful:

  • Reading: difficulty connecting letters and sounds, slow or effortful reading, avoiding reading aloud, frequent guessing at words.
  • Math: trouble understanding quantities, remembering number facts, or following multi-step procedures.
  • Writing: large gap between spoken ideas and written work, or very effortful handwriting and spelling.
  • Attention and organization: losing materials, unfinished work, difficulty starting tasks or following multi-step directions.
  • Emotional signs: stomach aches before school, “I’m stupid” talk, meltdowns around homework.

Step 1: Document what you see

Keep a simple log for two to four weeks: what task, what happened, how long it took, what helped. Collect work samples. Specific examples make conversations with teachers far more productive than general concerns.

Step 2: Talk with the teacher

Ask what they observe in class, how your child compares with grade expectations, and what support is already in place. Agree on one or two strategies to try, and a date to check in. Keep it collaborative: teachers are your strongest allies.

Step 3: Request an evaluation if concerns continue

Most school systems have a process to evaluate students who may need extra support. Put your request in writing, keep a copy, and ask about timelines. You may also seek a private evaluation from a qualified psychologist or specialist.

Understanding support plans

In the United States, eligible students may receive an IEP (Individualized Education Program), which provides specialized instruction and services, or a 504 plan, which provides accommodations such as extra time or preferential seating. Other countries use their own frameworks, often called individual education plans, special educational needs support or learning support plans. Ask your school what applies where you live.

Supporting your child at home

  • Lead with strengths. Make sure your child spends time every week on something they are good at.
  • Name it honestly and kindly. “Your brain learns reading differently, and we are going to find the way that works for you.”
  • Short, predictable routines for homework, with breaks and visual checklists.
  • Read aloud together so vocabulary and love of stories keep growing even while decoding catches up.
  • Look after yourself and connect with other parents; support groups reduce isolation.
